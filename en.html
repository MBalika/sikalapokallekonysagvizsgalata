Stability of shallow foundations

A footing can lose its stability in four ways: it slides on its base, overturns, floats up, or slips together with the surrounding soil. Each of the four has to be verified separately. On this page you can work through them with simulators, animations and quizzes, and see what decides each case.

In the top bar (verification panel) you can always see where the four simulators stand: the bar shows the utilisation, the mark is at 1.0.

Ed ≤ Rd

Four checks, one idea

Design value of the action ≤ design value of the resistance – for four different failure modes.

Together with sizing the footings – or after it – it must also be verified that the structure has adequate safety according to EC7 against sliding, overturning and uplift. In certain situations – near a slope, an excavation, a bank or a cavity – overall stability must be checked as well.

The procedure is the same for each: we write down the design value of the actions (Ed) and the design value of the resistance (Rd), then verify that Ed ≤ Rd. For overturning we compare moments: Mdst;d ≤ Mstb;d. Where the soil strength matters, both the drained and the undrained states are checked.

The ratio of the two is the utilisation: Λ = Ed / Rd. If Λ ≤ 1.0, the footing satisfies that limit state. The verification panel shows the same.

GEO

Sliding on the base

If the load is not perpendicular to the base, its horizontal component is resisted by shear under the base and by passive earth resistance.

Inclined loads act, for example, on the footings of retaining walls and bridge abutments (earth pressure), at the column bases of frames (horizontal reaction) or on structures exposed to wind. In such cases the risk of “sliding failure” must also be checked:

Hd ≤ Rd + Rp;d
Hd – design value of the horizontal force acting on the base, including the active earth pressure transferred to the footing
Rd – design value of the shear resistance on the base that prevents sliding
Rp;d – design value of the passive earth resistance on the side of the footing opposite to Hd

Rd and Rp;d must be chosen consistently with the movements expected in the limit state considered: for large displacements the post-peak behaviour of the shear strength is typical.

In design approach DA-2* according to the Hungarian National Annex of EN 1997-1, actions are taken with the A1 factors (γG = 1.35, γQ = 1.5), and the sliding resistance is reduced by the partial factor γR;h = 1.1.

The resistance has to be determined for two states: the drained state in the long term (effective stresses, friction) and, in cohesive soil, the undrained state in the short term (cu). The less favourable of the two governs.

Case 1: drained state – long term

Rd = V′k · tan δk / γR;h
V′k – characteristic value of the vertical force; a variable action only if it surely acts together with Hd
δk – interface friction angle between footing and soil, from the critical state φ′cv;k
γR;h = 1.1

For a footing cast in situ the base is rough and the grains interlock: δk = φ′cv;k. For a smooth precast footing only δk = ⅔·φ′cv;k may be assumed.

The effective cohesion (c′) should generally be ignored: the adhesion that develops between cohesive soil and the footing depends strongly on the water content and consistency.

Cast in situ or precast?

For a footing cast in situ δ = φ′cv;k, for a precast one only ⅔·φ′cv;k.
Example: cantilever wall, V′k = 162 kN/m, Hd = 65 kN/m.

Case 2: undrained state – short term

Rd = Ac · cu;k / γR;h
Ac – base area in compression (for an eccentric load A′ = B′ · L′)
cu;k – characteristic value of the undrained shear strength at the base
Rd ≤ 0.4 · Vd
No greater shear resistance may be assumed than 40 per cent of the design value of the vertical force on the footing.

A gap may form between footing and soil, and water or air may reach the base. Therefore the 0.4·Vd limit must also be checked. It may only be omitted if suction prevents a gap from forming even where there is no positive contact pressure – this is rarely the case.

Example: Ac = 2.0 m²/m, cu;k = 60 kPa → Ac·cu;k/1.1 = 109 kN/m; Vd = 180 kN/m → 0.4·Vd = 72 kN/m, so Rd = 72 kN/m – the limit governs.

Passive resistance – Rp;d

The full passive earth resistance is mobilised only after a significant displacement, so typically only a part of it, one half to two thirds, may be taken into account, with the partial factor γR;e = 1.4.

It must also be borne in mind that erosion or some human intervention (e.g. a later utility trench) may remove the soil in front of the footing face, and in clay the soil may separate from the face because of seasonal changes in water content. If this cannot be ruled out, we conservatively take Rp;d = 0.

Some design software offers to take only ½–⅓ of the full passive earth resistance into account.

If it fails

Structural options

  • A shear key under the base: it forces the slip surface lower, into the soil, with passive resistance developing in front of it.
  • More self-weight, a wider base – more soil on the heel: a larger V′k.
  • A deeper foundation level – larger, more reliable passive resistance.
  • A rough base cast in situ instead of a precast one.

Careful with shear keys

Experience shows that the benefit of a shear key is mixed: in strong soil and with a smooth precast base it helps a lot; with a shallow key, however, once the active pressure on it is also counted, the safety may even get worse (in one worked example from 1.39 to 1.24). Count on the passive resistance down to the bottom of the key only if it will surely remain.

1911

Austin Dam, Pennsylvania, USA

In January 1910, after rapid filling, the 15 m high gravity dam slid on its foundation: about 46 cm at the toe and about 79 cm at the crest. The slip surface formed along clay-filled bedding joints, and the uplift of water getting under the dam reduced the safety further. On 30 September 1911 the dam failed – 78 people died.

Quiz – sliding

EQU

Overturning and eccentricity

With a highly eccentric load, the destabilising moment must not reach the stabilising one.

If the load is transferred to the base with a large eccentricity, there is a risk of the footing and the supported structure overturning. This is treated as loss of static equilibrium (EQU) and checked by comparing the destabilising moments with those resisting them:

Mdst;d ≤ Mstb;d
Both moments are calculated from the design values of the actions.
Permanent: γG;dst = 1.1; γG;stb = 0.9
Variable destabilising: γQ;dst = 1.5
Variable favourable: 0

About which point do we take moments?

For a footing on sound rock, about the toe (toe line) of the footing. For a footing on soil – since the soil under the toe is pressed in – as a first approximation about a point B/10 inside the toe. You can try both in the 3D simulator.

Limiting the eccentricity

The standard requires special precautions if the eccentricity of the load exceeds 1/3 of the width of a rectangular footing or 0.6 times the radius of a circular one. The contact pressure then concentrates on one side of the footing, the edge pressure increases considerably, and the effective base area that can be counted on for bearing resistance decreases – half of the base no longer “works”.

In design approach DA-2* the eccentricity must be determined from the characteristic values of the actions; the partial factors do not change it.

Earlier Hungarian practice, before EC7, was stricter: the whole base had to “work”, and the eccentricity was limited to B/6 – the resultant had to pass through the base within the kern.

Short-term or permanent?

It matters whether the overturning action is short-term (e.g. wind load) or permanent (e.g. earth pressure). In a gust the base lifts off temporarily and then settles back; under a permanent eccentric load the footing may gradually tilt. The German guideline for wind turbines therefore allows no gap under quasi-permanent loading, and under extreme loading a gap at most up to the centre.

If it fails

  • a larger base – the lever arm and the self-weight grow, e/B decreases;
  • a deeper footing – more soil cover, more self-weight;
  • shifting the base under the resultant (eccentric footing).
On soil the GEO check also matters. In EC7 terms, overturning as a rigid body (EQU) is rare in geotechnics and governs mainly for footings on rock; on soil a large eccentricity also shows up in the bearing resistance check (with the effective width B′ = B − 2e).

Quiz – overturning

UPL

Uplift

With a high groundwater table, a light structure of large volume may be acted on by an uplift force greater than the sum of the permanent loads.

In design practice it happens countless times that a light but “voluminous” structure – an underground tank, a pedestrian underpass, an underground car park – is acted on by a greater uplift force at high groundwater than the sum of the permanent loads. The Eurocode defines a separate ultimate limit state for this: UPL (uplift).

Vdst;d ≤ Gstb;d + Rd
Vdst;d = γG;dst · Ak · γw – the uplift force (Ak: the volume submerged below the groundwater, γw = 10 kN/m³)
Gstb;d = Gstr;d + Gsoil;d – the weight of the structure and of the soil loading it, with γG;stb
Rd = Td + Pd – friction between the structure and the soil, and the anchor force

The utilisation: Λ = Vdst;d / (Gstb;d + Rd).

UPL – partial factors on actions
γG;dst1.00 – permanent, destabilising (uplift force)
γG;stb0.90 – permanent, stabilising (self-weight)
γQ;dst1.50 – variable, destabilising
γQ;stb0 – variable, stabilising

The overall safety against uplift is usually only γG;dst / γG;stb = 1.0 / 0.9 = 1.11. This is why it is particularly important to choose the water levels cautiously, or to introduce precautions that reduce the risk.

For the resistance of friction and anchors EN 1997-1 recommends γs;t = γa = 1.4 (since the AC:2010 corrigendum they may be treated as stabilising permanent actions only if the National Annex allows it). The values of the Hungarian NA are worth checking in the standard.

The groundwater level is the most sensitive input

When buildings and structures are checked against uplift, the groundwater level is treated as a geometrical datum: its design value is either set directly or derived by adding a safety margin to the characteristic value. In the 30×15 m example car park, water 1 m higher means +4500 kN, i.e. +20% uplift force.

On a site near the Danube a long-lasting flood is followed by the groundwater along the bank as well: in South Buda after the 1965 flood the groundwater along the bank rose by more than 3 m and came close to the maximum level of the river. The design flood level of Budapest (MÁSZ) at Vigadó tér is 104.24 mBf – derived from the discharge of the ice-free flood with a 1% probability.

Four countermeasures

a)

Extending the base slab

Beyond the walls, if there is room: the soil resting on the toe adds load as well. In the example, with a 1 m extension a 0.67 m slab is enough instead of 1.28 m – but the uplift force acts on the projecting toe too.

b)

Anchoring

With piles, diaphragm wall panels or ground anchors. With a 0.6 m slab about 6300 kN is missing: overall at least 15 GEWI micropiles with a characteristic resistance of 600 kN each – because of the bending of the slab, a denser grid in practice.

c)

Lowering the groundwater

Around the structure, during rare high water (e.g. the flood wave of a nearby river), or reducing the water pressure under the structure by drainage. Only with a monitored system: the pump may stop, the filter may clog.

d)

Temporary flooding

Part of the structure – e.g. the lowest level of an underground car park – is filled with water. In the example 2 m of water is about 8300 kN of ballast: with the 0.6 m slab Λ = 0.95.

Cases

2013

Kossuth Square, Budapest – flooding the excavation

During a Danube flood, the excavation of the four-level underground car park still under construction would not have withstood the increased groundwater pressure, so about 60 000 m³ of water was let into it, to a depth of 8 m; after the flood had passed it was pumped out. The state during construction is often less favourable than the final one.

2015

Oldsmar, Florida – a pool popping up

After about 16 days of rain, the rim of a pool rose out of the ground first by 48 cm, then by 99 cm. Australian official guidance warns specifically that an empty pool, especially a fibreglass one, may “pop out” of the ground after a flood.

19 cm

Underground car park, southern China

The base slab of the parking basement under a tower block rose by 0.194 m after heavy rain, poor drainage and loose backfill; about 47 columns cracked. Repair: pressure relief holes through the slab, pumping, additional load.

≈1985

Scaramanga dry dock, Greece

The 1.2 m thick floor slab, held down by prestressed anchors, rose like a dome and cracked after the anchor strands broke by stress corrosion – the groundwater flooded the dock. It was then rebuilt with a pressure-relieved floor.

Quiz – uplift

GEO

Overall stability

The footing and the surrounding soil mass are checked together – the bearing resistance may be adequate while the slope is not.

For shallow foundations, overall stability has to be checked mainly when the footing is placed at one of the following locations:

  • on or near a natural or man-made slope;
  • near an excavation or a retaining wall;
  • near a river bank, canal, lake, reservoir or the sea shore;
  • on undermined ground or near underground structures.

If there is a chance of loss of stability, it must be shown by an analytical or numerical method that the probability of failure of the soil mass containing the loaded footing is acceptably small. Loss of stability is usually not caused by a single footing: the combined load of several footings triggers the movement of a sloping or undermined soil mass.

Slip surface analysis

It is verified by a slope stability calculation: a circular slip surface (in layered soil, other shapes if needed), the method of slices, a search for the most unfavourable slip surface. The load of the footing enters as an action on the slices it stands above. Both the drained (c′, φ′) and the undrained (cu) states are checked; the water conditions usually govern.

DA-3: γG = 1.0 · γQ = 1.3
tan φ′d = tan φ′k / γφ, c′d = c′k / γc
γφ = γc = 1.35; the M2 value recommended by EN 1997-1 is 1.25 (for cu, γcu = 1.4).

Solutions

  • moving the footing further away from the crest;
  • founding deeper – below the slip surface;
  • flattening or supporting the slope;
  • dewatering, sorting out the surface drainage.

How far is “far”?

According to Meyerhof’s tests, a slope reduces the bearing resistance up to a distance of about 2–6·B. The US building code requires H/3 (at most 12.2 m) on the crest of a descending slope and H/2 (at most 4.6 m) at the toe of an ascending slope – for stable slopes only. These are indicative values; the verification is the slip surface calculation.

Cases

2011

Kulcs – loess bank

53 properties were put at risk and at least 14 buildings had to be demolished; experts had warned in advance, but there was no money to strengthen the bank.

2008

Dunaszekcső, Hold-hegy

In February 2008 the 70 m high loess bluff slid and sank by about 10 m, and a shoal formed in the bed of the Danube. Research shows that the movement is driven mainly by changes in the groundwater level – its effect is about ten times that of the Danube water level.

1914

Balatonkenese – high bluff

After a wet period, about 100 000 m³ of loess swept a train into Lake Balaton; the driver stopped in time, and there were no casualties.

1972

Po Shan Road, Hong Kong

Rain of more than 200 mm a day on three consecutive days, and a 13 m deep unsupported cut at the toe of the slope: about 80 000 t of soil slid down in 7–10 seconds, and a 12–13 storey block of flats was torn off its foundation and collapsed. 67 people died.

2009

Lotus Riverside, Shanghai

A 13-storey building on piles toppled over almost intact: an underground car park pit was dug on one side, and the excavated soil was heaped about 10 m high on the other side, on the creek bank. It was a piled building, and a later analysis disputes the official cause – but it shows the danger of “fill next to the building” well.

Contrast: Transcona, 1913. The Winnipeg grain elevator tilted to about 27° within 24 hours – but this was a bearing failure of the soil under the raft (bearing resistance, GEO), not a loss of overall stability and not overturning as a rigid body.

Quiz – overall stability

DA

Design approaches and factors

EN 1997-1 and its Hungarian National Annex: which set of factors applies to which verification.

Eurocode 7 gives separate groups of partial factors for actions (A), soil parameters (M) and resistances (R), and combines them into three design approaches (DA-1, DA-2, DA-3). The design approach is chosen by the National Annex.

In Hungarian practice DA-2* is used for the bearing resistance and sliding of shallow foundations, and DA-3 for slopes and overall stability.

ApproachCombinationUse in Hungary
DA-11.: A1 + M1 + R1

DA-2*A1 + M1 + R2bearing resistance and sliding of shallow foundations
DA-3A1 / A2 + M2 + R3slopes, overall stability

What does the asterisk in DA-2* mean?

The calculation runs with characteristic values throughout, and the partial factors are applied at the end, to the effects of actions and to the resistance. This is why the eccentricity and the effective base area (B′ = B − 2e) are also calculated from the characteristic actions.

The groups of factors

  • A1: γG = 1.35; γQ = 1.5 · A2: γG = 1.0; γQ = 1.3
  • M1: all soil parameters unfactored (1.0)
  • M2: tan φ′ and c′ divided, for cu γcu = 1.4
  • R2 (shallow foundation): γR;v = 1.4 bearing, γR;h = 1.1 sliding
  • R3: slopes, overall stability: 1.0

Equilibrium limit states – with their own factors

Loss of static equilibrium (EQU), uplift (UPL) and hydraulic heave (HYD) are not tied to the design approaches: Annex A of EN 1997-1 gives a separate set of factors for each of the three.

    2G

    What changes in the new Eurocode 7?

    The limit states remain – the assignment of factors changes.

    For information. On this page every explanation, worked example, simulator and calculator follows the current EN 1997-1 and its Hungarian National Annex. This chapter only shows what will change with the second-generation standard.

    Now – verification by DA-2* and DA-3

    • EQU: separate factors for stabilising and destabilising actions (γG;stb = 0.9; γG;dst = 1.1)
    • sliding, bearing resistance: DA-2* (A1 + M1 + R2)
    • soil parameters at characteristic values, the resistance reduced by γR
    • overall stability: DA-3

    New – verification cases + MFA / RFA

    • equilibrium and uplift: VC2
    • shallow foundations, slopes, retaining walls: VC3 (MFA – with a factor on tan φ′ and c′)
    • RFA: the resistance with γR (bearing ≈ 1.4, sliding 1.1); within RFA, VC1 or VC4 (≈ DA-2*)
    • the values come from the National Annex – not yet published

    For shallow foundations

    • The bearing resistance formula has moved into the main text, probably with a ground inclination factor.
    • Overturning (static equilibrium, without ground failure) and toppling (rotational bearing failure) are checked separately.
    • The eccentricity limit is e ≤ B/3, 0.59·r for a circular footing; in the serviceability state no gap should open at the edge of the footing.
    • The 0.4 limit for undrained sliding remains; the factors for sliding and bearing resistance do not change numerically (1.1 and 1.4).

    New concepts

    • Ground Model → Geotechnical Design Model: a mandatory line of reasoning.
    • The Geotechnical Category results from the consequence class (CC) combined with the complexity of the ground conditions (GCC).
    • Overall stability moves into Clause 4 of EN 1997-3 (slopes, cuttings, embankments); the zone of influence is a new concept.
    • Representative values instead of characteristic ones.
